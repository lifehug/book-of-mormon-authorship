# Who wrote the Book of Mormon? What we did, explained simply

*Draft 1, 2026-11-10. No background needed. We don't argue about whether the book is from God;
that's not something a computer can test. We asked a smaller question: does its writing look like
any particular person's writing, did it copy from other books, and how old does its language
sound?*

## Everyone has a writing fingerprint

When you write, you leave a fingerprint without meaning to. Not the big words; the tiny ones. How
often you say "the", "of", "which", "upon". Whether you start sentences with "And". These habits
are hard to fake, because you don't notice them.

Computers can count these habits very well. If two pieces of writing have the same habits, the
same person may have written both. That's how people have identified who wrote some of the
anonymous *Federalist Papers*, for example.

## Building a computer that lives in 1830

Today's AI chatbots read the whole internet, so they know modern English. That's no good for a book
from 1830. So we built our own: we fed computers only books printed between 1780 and 1830, about
200,000 of them, and nothing newer. We built three of these "time-traveller" brains, small,
medium and large.

Because they only know 1800s English, they're good at noticing what was normal back then, and what
sounded unusual.

We never let them read the Book of Mormon while learning. That's like not letting a student see
the test answers.

## Taking the test away from the Bible

About 7 out of every 100 words in the Book of Mormon come straight from the King James Bible. If we
left those in, every test would just say "sounds like the Bible!" So we covered up every
Bible-copied part first, and only looked at the rest.

## Practising on puzzles we already knew the answers to

Before the real thing, we gave every method practice tests where we *knew* who the writer was,
including tricky ones like people pretending to write like the Bible. That told us how often each
method gets it right, and how often it falsely says "it's this person!" when it isn't.

The best method picked the right writer 98 times out of 100. But it still said "it's this
person!" wrongly about 1 time in 5. So nothing we have is perfect, and we say so.

## Writing the rules before playing the game

Before looking at the answer, we wrote down every rule: what counts as a match, how sure we need to
be. We posted those rules publicly. That way, nobody (including us) could change the rules
afterwards to get the answer they wanted.

## The blind test

We chopped the Book of Mormon into 168 pieces, then mixed in 43 "decoy" pieces: some written by
the suspected authors, some by people who weren't suspects at all. The computers judged all 211
pieces without knowing which was which. Only afterwards did we open the answer key.

## What we found

**1. The tools work.** On the decoys, the computers usually found the real writer, and they never
once wrongly blamed a non-suspect.

**2. No suspect matched.** We tested the people others have suggested: Solomon Spalding, Sidney
Rigdon, Oliver Cowdery, Ethan Smith, Parley Pratt, Joseph Smith and others. None of them was a
confident match. Our combined score said "probably none of these people" about 74 out of 100.

**3. We searched the whole library too.** We compared the book against almost 24,000 writers from
that time. No one came close enough to count.

**4. It didn't copy other books.** Books people have suggested as sources, like *View of the
Hebrews* and Spalding's story, looked like ordinary books from that time. A couple of books that
also imitated the Bible shared some old-fashioned phrases, but so did that whole style of writing.
We found no copied passage.

**5. It sounds old, older than it should.** Our "how old does this sound?" tool said most of the
book sounds like English from before 1700, and older than other people who imitated the Bible in
the early 1800s.

**6. When forced to pick, the computers leaned toward Joseph Smith or Oliver Cowdery,** the man
who spoke the book aloud and the man who wrote most of it down. But we only had very short samples
of their early writing, too short to trust, so this doesn't count as a match.

## What this doesn't tell you

It doesn't tell you how the book was made, whether it's a translation, or whether it's true.
Computers can't test that. It tells you this: by the best tools we could build, the book's writing
doesn't confidently match any of the people usually suggested, and it doesn't look copied.

## Check our work

Everything is free to see and rerun: our rules, our programs, our results, and the computer
brains themselves. If we made a mistake, anyone can find it. That's the whole point.
