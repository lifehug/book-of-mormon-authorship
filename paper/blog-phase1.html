Phase 1 · the write-up

Who wrote the Book of Mormon? I asked the text, and here's what it said.

Dave Taylor · draft of 13 November 2026
Draft for my own review. Every number here comes from a results file in the repo. The charts are live: hover or tap the bars, click the buttons.

In 2016 a friend and I wrote a little Java program that counted the words in the 1830 Book of Mormon and compared them with Joseph Smith's journals. It was a weekend thing. It answered nothing, and the repo sat for ten years.

This year I picked it back up, with better tools and one rule: write down how every test will be judged before I see its result. I'm not a scientist. What I can offer in place of credentials is that everything is public and anyone can run it again.

One thing up front. I'm not arguing about whether the book is inspired. A computer can't answer that, and I won't pretend it can. What it can do is tell me whether the English text looks like a particular person's writing, whether it borrows from particular books, and how far those tools can be trusted.

  1. The book copies almost nothing. Set the King James Bible aside and 0.16% of it matches earlier books. The books people name as sources don't stand out from other books of their kind.
  2. Of the eight people usually proposed, the closest writing is Joseph Smith's revelations. Six of eight methods rank them first, including all three of my models. His letters and journals don't stand out the same way.
  3. I can't tell you the author is one of those eight. None of the methods, mine included, can reliably say "it's nobody on this list". That's what Phase 2 is for.

1. The library

The first problem with asking a modern AI about the Book of Mormon is that it has already read the Book of Mormon, and everything written about it since. So I built language models that have read only what was in print before 1830.

695,021books, pamphlets and newspaper pages catalogued, 1780–1830
25.7 billiontokens of period text the models learned from
3model sizes, trained from scratch: 125M, 297M, 590M
8candidates with writing samples

How that compares with what's been tried before

Computer studies of this question go back to 1980. Four of them compared the Book of Mormon with a few dozen to a couple of hundred writing samples from a handful of people. One, in 2013, searched about 100,000 old books for shared phrases. As far as I can find, nothing on this question has come close to this size. Switch between the two measures.

Size isn't the only difference, and it may not be the main one.

The eight candidates are the ones people have argued about for two centuries: Joseph Smith, Oliver Cowdery, Sidney Rigdon, Parley Pratt, W. W. Phelps, Solomon Spalding, Ethan Smith and Gilbert Hunt.

The second problem is the Bible. In the 1830 text, the phrase it came to pass that appears 1,290 times. Any method that doesn't deal with that is measuring the King James Version. So King James wording is masked out of every text before any method sees it.

2. Did it borrow?

I started here because it's what the CES Letter and most online arguments are about: The Late War, View of the Hebrews, The First Book of Napoleon. I ran four tests, from the crudest to the subtlest.

Copied wording

I looked for passages the Book of Mormon shares, word for word or nearly, with any of 221,000 earlier books. I ran the same search on two other books so the number would mean something.

The Book of Mormon's 0.16% is 30 books sharing short, loose phrases, the religious talk of the day. "The Lord cannot look upon sin with the least degree of allowance" turns up in Bishop Ravenscroft. Nothing is a lifted page.

Rare shared phrases: The Late War

A 2013 study found that Gilbert Hunt's The Late War (1816), a history of the War of 1812 written in Bible style, shares rare four-word phrases with the Book of Mormon. I reran it by their own rule and got 183 phrases. Then I counted how many other books use each one.

Six are found nowhere else. Here are five of them, in both books. Click through.

The Late War and The First Book of Napoleon both pass the rule I'd written down. But a reprint of Jacob Ilive's Book of Jasher, a 1751 forgery in the same style, shares more than either. So what they share is a genre.

Same ideas, different words: View of the Hebrews

Ethan Smith's 1823 book argued that American Indians descend from the lost tribes of Israel. Compared passage by passage, it came out first of 49 books on that subject. That sounds like a hit. But against 500 equally religious books it lands at the 85th percentile, and my bar was the top 1%. Close in what it talks about, and well short of the bar.

Same story, new names

You can borrow a plot without sharing a sentence. So a model read every book in blocks and listed what happens, with the names swapped for roles. Here it is reading Genesis 45:

A ruler cries out for everyone else to leave the room. The ruler reveals himself to his brothers and weeps so loudly that the household hears. ... The father faints, not believing them, until he sees the wagons.

No Joseph, no Pharaoh, and you still know the story. I checked the test first on Josephus, who retold Genesis through Judges in his own words: it found 108 events in the same order. Then I ran it on the Book of Mormon. No book shares its plot.

What this section can't say. I ran the same tests on Macpherson's Ossian, a 1760s book written in its own day and presented as ancient. It copies nothing either (0.00%). So these tests can't tell a real translation from a period book written to sound old. "No borrowing found" describes the text. It says nothing about where it came from.

3. The earlier studies, re-run

Computer studies of this question go back to 1980, and they disagree. I rebuilt five of them with their own methods and data. Each reproduces. Each also turns on a choice nobody tested.

The 2008 Stanford study gave 169 chapters to Sidney Rigdon. Swap in other genuine Rigdon writing and it's 92. The 2011 study that allowed "none of the candidates" has an error rate that swings from 80% to 0% depending on how long the samples are. Same method, same people, different answer.

That's why the next section exists.

4. Are my tools any good?

Before any method touched the Book of Mormon, it had to pass an exam on books where I know the author. Every method takes the same exam: mine, and the four older ones people have used (Burrows' Delta, Cosine Delta, General Imposters, and a sparse-coding method called ENSC).

My method works like this. For each candidate, take a copy of the period model and let it read that person's writing. Then show it a mystery passage. The score is how much better the copy predicts the passage than the untouched model does. Whoever's copy improves the most is the pick.

Does training on period text matter?

Same test, same settings, only the base model swapped. A false match is the model saying "yes, that's my author" about someone it never read.

Picking the right author from a list

On 55 authors and over a thousand passages, my models beat every older method, at every size. I count by author, because twenty passages from one writer tend to go right or wrong together.

Saying "none of these"

This is where every method fails, mine too. My bar was 5% false matches while still confirming 95% of true authors. Held to that, my models give 51–57% false matches. Turn it round and aim for 5% false matches, and they confirm only 38–61% of true authors. I tried the standard fix from voice-ID research. Tested properly, on authors it had never seen, it didn't work.

So these tools are good at "which of these people" and bad at "is it any of these people". Everything below is the first kind of answer.

The biblical costume

One more weakness. I found three 18th-century writers who wrote both ordinary prose and imitation scripture. Trained on their ordinary prose, plain word counts named the right author on 13 of 18 scripture passages. My 297M model got 7 of 17. Three writers is too few to judge either way, and I looked hard for more: across the whole library only about ten people wrote imitation scripture, and three left enough other prose. But it's a warning, because the Book of Mormon wears that costume.

5. Joseph Smith, up close

First, what the revelations are

Joseph Smith left two very different kinds of writing. One is ordinary: letters, a journal, a short history of his life. The other is what he called revelations. These are short texts, dictated aloud to a scribe, in which the speaker is God. They were later gathered into the Doctrine and Covenants, which Latter-day Saints hold as scripture alongside the Bible and the Book of Mormon.

Seven of them survive from before the Book of Mormon was printed, 2,844 words in all. They were dictated in the same months as the book, to some of the same scribes. Another 95 followed between 1830 and 1834.

Here's why I keep the two kinds apart. Latter-day Saints believe the revelations came from God, and that the Book of Mormon was translated by that same gift and power. In that way of thinking, neither one is Joseph Smith's own composition, and the interesting comparison isn't the book against his letters. It's the book against the other texts said to come the same way. Someone who thinks he wrote both would want the same comparison, for a different reason: it's the nearest thing to his voice when he's writing scripture.

So every test below treats his revelations and his ordinary writing as separate samples, and I show the ranking both with the revelations and without them. I'm not saying which way of thinking is right. I'm making sure the test can speak to both.

The ranking

Here's the ranking itself. Each of the 168 sealed passages was scored against every candidate sample, by eight methods: my three models, two style encoders, and three older word-count methods. Some candidates have more than one sample, because a man's letters and his dictated revelations aren't the same kind of writing. Joseph Smith has four. Pick a method and see the list.

Six of the eight methods put the 1828–29 revelations first. Burrows' Delta puts his ordinary prose first and the revelations near the bottom. General Imposters puts Parley Pratt first and the revelations second. All three of my models, the ones that did best on the exam in section 4, are among the six.

The revelation sample is small: 3,039 tokens, a couple of pages. So I spent most of the remaining time trying to knock the result down. Pick a model size and see how each attempt went.

At the two smaller sizes the result survived everything I threw at it. At the largest size, writers who could not have been the author do as well, which means at that size the model is following the biblical style at least as much as the person. No size has earned more trust than the others on known answers, so I report it as a split.

Without the revelations

Take the revelations away and ask about his ordinary writing, the letters and journals. Now it depends on who you ask. The two word-count methods put his prose first. My language models put it mid-pack, with Cowdery, Phelps or his own letters first depending on size. The other methods name Cowdery or Pratt. There's no agreement.

Two readings

His revelation voice, from 1828 through 1834, is the nearest writing I have to the Book of Mormon. That fits "the same man". It also fits "the same kind of text": a revelation and a scripture are both dictated in a sacred voice, and his later revelations could echo the book. These tools can't separate the two, and I'm not going to pick for you.

One thing the manuscripts do show. The original manuscript was taken down as Joseph Smith spoke, and the printer's manuscript is a copy of it. If the original was written by ear, it should be full of misheard words like there for their. It is: with the same scribe on both, those slips are about nine times as common in the original (30 of 590 against 5 of 837). The book was dictated aloud. An AI read the page photos for that check, not a human expert.

6. The other candidates

Before reading anything into a low rank, I asked a simpler question. If a model learns a person from one of their works, does it recognise them in another?

Only Ethan Smith passes, and he isn't the closest to the Book of Mormon. For Cowdery, Rigdon, Phelps and Pratt, the models learned a book. So a low rank for them means "not shown", and I can't write "ruled out". Spalding and Hunt have one work each, so they can't be checked this way at all.

7. Everyone else

What about people nobody has proposed? I compared the Book of Mormon against all 23,872 authors in the library with at least 10,000 words, using the two methods cheap enough to run that many times. No one stood out. I'd worked out in advance that chance alone should throw up 0.035 false leads. I got zero.

But here's the catch, and it's a big one. On 200 books where I know the author, that same search puts the true author first only 27–35% of the time. My bar was 70%. So the search can say "nobody jumped out". It can't say "nobody's there". And my best method wasn't in it at all, because it needs one model per author.

8. What I got wrong

I promised to show the wrong turns. These are the ones that changed a result.

  • Cowdery's sample quoted the book. His 1829 "Articles of the Church" is 40% Book of Mormon, word for word. Of course it looked close. I now drop any candidate passage that shares 20 or more words with the book and was written after that part was dictated.
  • I counted passages when I should have counted authors. I'd reported my models beating word counts at p < 0.0001. Counted by author it was p 0.13. The claim only became true after I added 32 more authors, with a rule that I couldn't add any more after that.
  • A fix that graded its own homework. A quick version of the "none of these" fix looked like a breakthrough: three in four true authors confirmed. It had been learning each author's quirks, then testing on the same author. Done properly, it failed.
  • "The biggest model sees best." I wrote that, and it's wrong. The sizes tie at picking authors. A fourth, billion-parameter model failed in training and isn't used.
  • One lucky sample. Early rankings used one or two random slices of each writer. They now use up to twelve, spread evenly.

9. What's next

Phase 1 ends on an open door: the closest of eight, with no way to say whether the author is among the eight. Phase 2 goes after that. The goal is one sentence with a measured number in it: if the author left other writing in this library, this procedure finds him X% of the time.

The plan is two steps. A fast search narrows 23,872 authors to a shortlist. Then my per-author models, the ones that won the exam above, pick from the shortlist. The first thing I needed to know was how long that shortlist can be.

That's better than I feared. It isn't the answer yet: these 55 authors all left plenty of text, and the fast search still has to get the right person onto the shortlist in the first place.

10. Check it yourself

Everything is public: the code, the result tables, the plan and every change to it, and the base and candidate models on Hugging Face. With the candidate models you can rerun the Book of Mormon result without training anything. Four sources I'm not allowed to republish (HathiTrust, Harvard's book collection, the Joseph Smith Papers transcripts, and one purchased book), and for each the repo gives the steps to get the same access I had.

I did this as one person working with AI, and I want that to be plain. The reviews mentioned in the repo are simulated by an AI model and labelled that way. If you find a mistake, I'd like to know.
