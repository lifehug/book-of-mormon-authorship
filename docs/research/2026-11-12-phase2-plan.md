# Phase 2 plan: searching the whole library for a better candidate list

Written 2026-11-12 by an AI research agent (Claude), at the owner's request. This is a planning
document, not a result. Any review described here is simulated by an AI model. Nothing below has
been run on the Book of Mormon. The study takes no position on the book's origin; this plan is
about what the methods can and cannot do.

**How to read the labels.**

- **[repo]** checked today against a file in this repository (the file is named).
- **[lit]** from published literature (reference list at the end, with how each was verified).
- **[judgment]** my own reasoning; treat as a proposal.

**One disclosure first.** To write this I computed recall figures from the saved true-author
ranks in `data/sweep/calibration.json`, joined to names and counts in

## 0. The two-minute version

**What you asked.** Eight candidates is a small list. Can we search all 23,875 authors, get a
short list we trust, and rerun the careful Phase 1 analysis on it?

**Short answer.** Yes, as a staged search (wide and cheap first, narrow and expensive last). That
is the standard design for this problem. But three things have to be said plainly:

1. **A short list we can trust is not short today.** On the 200 known books, to catch the true
   author 80% of the time the two existing sweeps need a list of about 270 authors each. For 90%
   they need about 2,200 each. For 95%, about 6,800. A list of 10 catches the author 62% of the
   time. [repo] So "a short list that surely contains the author" does not exist yet; "a list of
   a few hundred that contains the author four times in five, for an ordinary library book" does.
2. **Those figures are for ordinary books, and they are a ceiling for the Book of Mormon.** The
   200 test books are compared with their author's other books of the usual kind. The Book of
   Mormon is in scriptural style, and its author may not be in the library at all (of the eight
   named candidates, only Gilbert Hunt appears in the swept list by name [repo]). No search can
   say "the author is not in the library".
3. **Running the same search many times does not help.** The cheap sweeps give the same answer
   every time. Repeats only help when something is random, and they only make the answer
   steadier, not more correct. What does help is combining *different kinds* of evidence, and
   even that has a limit here: the two sweeps we have fail on the same books (24 books missed by
   both, where independent failures would give about 6). [repo]

**The design I recommend.**

| Stage | What | Authors left | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | Clean the author labels (the same person appears under several names) | 23,875 labels, fewer people | Mac, free |
| 1 | All cheap sweeps, combined by a simple rank rule | about 2,000-4,000 | Mac, free |
| 2 | One model that knows every author (the planned Amendment 57 model) | about 50 | about $15-25 |
| 3 | Phase 1's own tools on those 50: one model per author, equal dose, placebo writers, plausibility | a ranked list with caveats | about $15-25 |

Each list size is picked from the known books, on half of them, and checked on the other half.
The end-to-end chance of the true author finishing first, for an ordinary book, is probably
between one in three and three in four; we will know after stage 2 is measured. For the Book of
Mormon it is lower, by an amount stage 1 can partly measure (books whose kind differs from their
author's other work).

**First three things to do.**

1. Clean the labels and decide the test books **before** the Amendment 57 training set is built
   (free, a day). Both are baked into that model; changing them later means retraining.
2. Combine the sweeps and measure "list of K contains the author" on a held-out half (free, hours).
3. Then the Amendment 57 model, with a wider shortlist than planned (about $15-25), and the
   list-size test (BOM-90) made larger and harder (about $10-15), because stage 3 depends on it.

**What a "better candidate" would mean.** An author who stays at or near the top under equal
dose at more than one model size, is not matched by writers who could not have written the book,
and has a documented path to the region. It would not mean that person wrote it. Writers who
imitate scripture will rank high for sounding biblical, and the list will be full of them.

## 1. Is a cascade the right approach?

### 1.1 What the literature does at this scale

**Large candidate sets are an established setting, and accuracy there is low at rank 1 and much
better at rank K.** [lit]

- Narayanan et al. (2012): 100,000 blog authors. The right author was first in about 20% of
  cases and in the top 20 in about 35%. Letting the system decline to answer raised precision
  from 20% to over 80% at half the recall, using the gap between the best and second-best score
  and whether two different classifiers agreed. They also note results are weaker when the
  unknown text comes from a different context than the known text.
- Koppel, Schler and Argamon (2011), and Koppel et al. (2006): thousands of candidate authors,
  where the author may be absent. Their answer is repeated comparison on random subsets of
  features, attributing only when one candidate wins consistently, and otherwise saying "don't
  know". This is the origin of General Imposters, which we already run. I could not open the
  full text today, so I give no figures from it.
- Luyckx and Daelemans (2011): accuracy falls steadily as the number of candidates grows and as
  text per author shrinks. This is why BOM-90 matters.
- Rivera-Soto et al. (2021, LUAR) and its successors treat the problem as retrieval: turn each
  author into a vector, rank by closeness, and report recall at 8 and mean reciprocal rank
  rather than top-1. Their finding that matters here: a model trained in one kind of writing
  transfers to another kind only sometimes. Our style encoder is this design, trained on period
  text.
- Agarwal et al. (2025) is the clearest published retrieve-then-rerank system for authorship.
  A fast retriever takes the top 100 from pools of 7,000-34,000 documents; a slower model that
  reads the query and each candidate together reranks them. The reranker added 6.2 and 8.5
  points of success-at-8 over the retriever alone (42.1 to 48.3, and 59.9 to 68.4). Two lessons:
  the second stage helps but modestly, and it can do *worse* than the first stage when trained
  carelessly (34.8 against 42.1 in one configuration).
- PAN shared tasks have mostly used small candidate lists (tens at most) or pairs of texts
  (verification), so they say little about 23,875 candidates. [lit, from memory]
- Tyo et al. (2022): with little text per author, simple word and character counts beat neural
  models. Consistent with keeping the classical sweeps in stage 1.

**What fits us and what does not.** [judgment]

| Idea | Fits? | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Retrieve wide, rerank narrow (cascade) | **Yes** | Our best tool costs money per author; the cheap ones do not. This is exactly the case a cascade is for. |
| Report recall at K, not only top-1 | **Yes** | The shortlist's job is to contain the author, not to name them. The registered 70% top-1 bar stays and is reported; it answers a different question ("can the sweep name someone?"). |
| Decline to answer using the gap and agreement between methods | **Yes, cheaply** | Narayanan's trick. We already save the best-false-match distance; the gap between first and second is one line of code. |
| Modern rerankers (LLMs reading both texts) | **No** | They need models trained on modern text, which have read the Book of Mormon. Our time-locked models are the only rerankers we can use, and that is what stages 2 and 3 are. |
| LUAR and other modern style vectors | **No** | Same reason; already set aside in the earlier report. |
| A model per author for everyone | **No** | About $10,000 (`docs/part2-library-search-plan.md`). |

### 1.2 Combining several sweeps (rank fusion)

**What it is.** Each sweep gives every author a rank. Reciprocal rank fusion (Cormack, Clarke
and Büttcher 2009) adds up 1/(60 + rank) across sweeps and re-sorts. Borda count adds the ranks
themselves. Neither needs training, so there is nothing to overfit on 200 books. [lit]

**What it can buy here.** Less than one would hope, and we can already see why. [repo]

- On the 200 books, Cosine Delta misses the top 1,000 on 44 books and the style encoder on 28.
  Both miss on 24. If their failures were unrelated we would expect about 6. Across books, the
  two methods' ranks for the true author correlate at 0.85.
- So today's "either method" figure (88% at 1,000 each) is only 2 points above the style encoder
  alone (86%).
- The interim log of the older methods (50 of 200 books, not final) shows the same pattern:
  the books where Cosine Delta puts the true author around 20,000th are the books where Burrows'
  Delta and ENSC do too. All three are built from the same 300 word frequencies, so this is
  expected. They are three readings of one instrument, not three instruments.

**Conclusion.** Fuse them, because it is free and helps a little at the top of the list. Do not
expect fusion to rescue the books every method misses. The signal most likely to add something
new is the labelled language model, because it reads word order, not word counts. [judgment]

**Do not learn fusion weights.** With 200 books, a weighted or trained combiner will fit noise.
The earlier normalisation episode (Amendment 55: looked good split by test, failed split by
author) is the same trap. Use the unweighted rule, fixed in advance.

### 1.3 A shortlist with a stated chance of containing the author (conformal prediction)

**The idea.** Take known cases. For each, record how far down the list the true answer was.
To get a list that contains the answer 90% of the time, cut the list where 90% of the known
cases would have been caught (with a small correction for the number of known cases). For a new
case *of the same kind*, the list then contains the answer at least 90% of the time. This is
split conformal prediction (Vovk et al. 2005; Angelopoulos and Bates 2021; Sadinle, Lei and
Wasserman 2019; Romano, Sesia and Candès 2020). Fisch et al. (2021) extend it to cascades: each
stage prunes with its own error allowance, and the allowances add up to the total. [lit]

**It fits our data exactly: we have 200 known cases with saved ranks.** Computed today [repo]:

| Wanted: list contains the true author | List size per sweep (both sweeps, lists joined) | Style encoder alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 70% of the time | 56 | 74 |
| 80% | 272 | 301 |
| 85% | 667 | 849 |
| 90% | 2,186 | 3,129 |
| 95% | 6,807 | 9,556 |

The steep climb after 85% is the hard core of books that every method misses.

**How firm are these?** Not very, at the high end. Choosing K on a random 100 books and testing
on the other 100, repeated 2,000 times: aiming for 80% gives K around 272 (ranging 128 to 654)
and delivers 70-89% on the test half; aiming for 90% gives K around 2,700 (ranging 850 to
5,800) and delivers 83-96%. [repo] With 100 test books every percentage carries roughly plus or
minus 6 points. More test books would tighten this (section 3.2).

**The limit, which must go in every sentence that uses the word "guarantee".** The promise
holds only if the new case is like the known cases. Ours is not, in three ways:

1. The Book of Mormon is in scriptural style and the candidates' profiles mostly are not.
   Methods for adjusting conformal sets to a shift exist (Tibshirani et al. 2019; Barber et al.
   2023), but they need to know how the new case differs in measurable terms, and they cannot
   cover "the author is not in the library".
2. The 200 books were drawn only from authors with at least two works. Two thirds of the
   library's authors have one work. [repo]
3. The author may be absent. Then the list contains the author 0% of the time, whatever its
   size.

So the honest statement is conditional: *"if the author is in the library and the book behaves
like an ordinary book by that author, a list this long contains them N% of the time."* It is
still worth having, because it replaces "top 1,000 sounds like plenty" with a measured number,
and because section 3.3 shows how to measure part of the shift.

## 2. Run once, or many times?

The owner's question: can we "run multiple times or over time to statistically get the right
person"? It depends on what is repeated. [judgment, with standard statistics]

| What is repeated | What it gives | What it cannot give |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The same sweep again** (Cosine Delta, Burrows, ENSC, the style encoder) | Nothing. These are arithmetic on fixed numbers; the answer is identical every time. | - |
| **General Imposters with other random draws** | A steadier score. It already averages 100 random trials inside. | A different answer on average. |
| **The labelled model with several training seeds** | How much the list moves from luck in training. If the top 50 changes a lot between seeds, the list is not trustworthy at that length. Averaging seeds removes that luck. | It does not remove a lean. If the model favours long-winded authors or biblical ones, every seed favours them. |
| **Resampling the query's passages** (bootstrap: draw the book's chunks again with replacement, re-rank) | How much the list depends on which passages happened to be used. Authors who stay in the top K in, say, 90% of resamples are the stable core. | Passages from one book are not independent witnesses (the Amendment 55 lesson). Resampling them understates the true uncertainty and cannot detect that the whole book is in a register the profiles lack. |
| **Different profile blocks for each author** (another seeded draw of the 20 blocks) | Whether an author ranks high because of one unusual block. Useful for authors with much more than 20,000 words. | Nothing for the 14% of authors with fewer than 20 blocks, who have no other draw. [repo] |
| **Different kinds of evidence** (word counts, style vectors, language model) | The only repetition that can raise accuracy, and only to the extent their mistakes differ. Section 1.2: for the two we have, they mostly do not. | Agreement among methods that share a blind spot looks like confirmation and is not. |

**The rule of thumb.** Repeats shrink the random part of the error. They leave the systematic
part untouched, and they make a systematic error look more certain. With a text whose main
difficulty is systematic (register), repeats are a stability check, not a route to the answer.

**"Tournament" versus one full ranking.** If each author's score does not depend on who else is
on the list, a tournament over sub-groups gives the same winner as one full ranking, so it
buys nothing but bookkeeping. That is true of our language-model scores (the passage's loss
under the author's model, less the base model's). It is not true of Burrows' Delta, Cosine
Delta or ENSC, whose scaling depends on the whole gallery, or of General Imposters, which
depends on the imposter pool. For those, rank on the whole gallery once.

**What to measure on the 200 known books** (all free, on the Mac, after the rule is written):

1. **Stability.** For each book, resample its query chunks 200 times. Record how often each
   author stays in the top K. Report the average overlap of the top-K lists between resamples.
2. **Does instability flag errors?** Compare the stability of books where the true author was
   caught with books where they were not. If unstable lists are the wrong ones, stability is a
   usable warning light; if not, say so.
3. **The gap.** The distance between the first and second author, against whether the first
   was right (Narayanan's confidence measure). We already save `best_false`; this is nearly free.
4. **Agreement.** When two sweeps name the same first author, how often is that author right?
   Today both rank the true author first on 22.5% of books [repo]; what is not saved is how
   often they agree on a wrong one. That needs the full rankings, which the sweep recomputes in
   minutes.
5. **Seeds for the labelled model** (GPU): two seeds, about $5 more. Report the overlap of the
   two top-50 lists per book. This is the one repeat worth paying for.

## 3. The design

### 3.1 Stage 0: clean the labels (and find out why some true authors rank 23,000th)

**Why first.** The author labels are catalog strings, and one person often has several. [repo]

- Joanna Southcott has three labels; Adam Clarke five; John Bunyan three; Richard Snowden two
  (142,035 and 141,809 words, so very likely the same book twice).
- 1,154 labels end in "from old catalog". Stripping only cataloguing suffixes ("from old
  catalog", "author", "comp", authority numbers) collapses 23,875 labels to 21,470, with 2,078
  groups of two or more labels covering 4,483 labels. That is a floor on the problem: it does
  not catch initials against full names, and it will wrongly join some different people of the
  same name (the Amendment 56 screen found three John Brands).

**Why it matters three times over.**

1. **The scorecard is too harsh.** If a test book's author also sits in the library under a
   second label, that second label can rank first and be counted as a wrong answer. On my crude
   rule, books whose author has a twin label have the true author first 29% of the time (49
   books) against 36% without (151 books). That gap is within noise, so cleaning will probably
   add a few points, not transform the result. [repo, exploratory]
2. **Leakage.** If the held-out book itself sits under the twin label, the "wrong" answer is the
   book matching itself. Amendment 57's 5-gram check is aimed at this; cleaning the labels
   first makes it mostly unnecessary.
3. **The labelled model.** One label token per catalog string means a split author's evidence is
   divided between labels that compete with each other. This is baked in at training time.

**The 24 worst books.** On 24 of 200 books neither sweep puts the true author in the top 1,000;
on 14, not in the top 5,000; the worst are 17,514th, 21,682nd and 22,949th. [repo] Nothing in
the saved counts explains them: they are not the authors with thin profiles (none of the 24 has
fewer than 10 blocks), not the short books (9 of 24 have three or fewer query chunks, against
55 of 200 overall), and not the authors left with a single other work (8 of 24, against 61 of
200). So the cause is in the content. The candidates, in the order I would check them:

1. **Wrong attribution in the catalog** (a different person of the same name; an editor,
   translator or compiler filed as author).
2. **A different kind of book** from the author's other works (a physician's poems; a
   dictionary against sermons). The genre label for every volume already exists.
3. **A different language, or a translation.**
4. **Bad OCR** in the book or the profile.

**How to do it.** For each of the 24: titles, years and genre labels of the held-out book and of
the profile works, from the catalog (names and metadata only), sorted into the four causes plus
"unexplained". An AI model can sort them from catalog rows; the result is labelled as an AI
judgment and committed as a table. **Cost: Mac, free, a few hours** including the label merge
and a rerun of the sweep (minutes).

**Rule to write first** (amendment): the merge rule (which suffixes are stripped; that life
dates, where present, must not conflict; that every merge is listed in a public table); that
the sweep is reported both before and after; and that no book is dropped from the 200. If a
cause turns out to be "the catalog is wrong about who wrote it", the book stays in and is
flagged: the Book of Mormon search runs on the same catalog.

### 3.2 The test books: split them, and consider more

Three things in this design get chosen from known answers: the merge rule's effect, the list
size at each stage, and whether fusion is used. If they are chosen and scored on the same 200
books, the reported hit rate is flattering. [judgment]

- **Minimum:** a seeded 100/100 split. Everything is chosen on the first half and reported on
  the second. Cost: wider error bars (about plus or minus 6-10 points).
- **Better, and nearly free for the cheap sweeps:** draw more test books. The word-count sweeps
  can use any author with two or more works (about a third of 23,875). The style encoder can
  only be tested on authors it never trained on: 854 were held out, of whom 200 are used.
  [repo: `data/style_embed/authors.parquet`] Several hundred more are probably available.
- **The catch, and the reason this is urgent:** the labelled model must never see a test book.
  Test books are removed from their author's profile *before* the training set is built. So the
  number of test books has to be decided before BOM-89 step 1, not after.

**Recommendation:** add as many style-held-out books as qualify under the registered rule
(same seed order, continuing down the list, as Amendment 56 did for the benchmark), keep the
original 200 as the reported set for the registered 70% bar, and use the new books for choosing
list sizes. Then nothing chosen touches the 200.

### 3.3 Stage 1: every cheap sweep, fused (Mac, free)

- **Signals:** Cosine Delta, Burrows' Delta, ENSC, the style encoder. General Imposters only
  reorders a top-100, so it is not a library-wide signal; leave it out of the fusion and report
  it alone.
- **Fusion:** reciprocal rank fusion with the standard constant 60, unweighted, fixed in advance.
  Report each signal alone, the fused list, and "joined lists" (today's method), side by side.
- **List size K1:** the split-conformal size for a stated coverage, from the choosing books,
  checked on the others. Today's numbers suggest aiming at 90%, which means a few thousand
  authors (about 2,200 from each of two sweeps, before cleaning or fusion). That is fine:
  stage 2 can afford it (section 3.4).
- **Measure the register shift directly.** Split the test books into those whose genre label
  matches most of the author's profile and those where it differs. Report recall at K for each.
  The second group is the nearest thing we have to the Book of Mormon's situation, and its
  recall is the more honest figure to carry forward. Also report the few scripture-style
  authors with several works (Southcott has 11 works, Brothers 6 [repo]) as a named handful,
  too few for a percentage.
- **Positive control.** Add the eight named candidates' Phase 1 samples to the gallery as
  profiles, flagged (most are under the 10,000-word floor; the 1828-29 revelations are 3,039
  tokens). Phase 1 found the revelations closest among eight. If a search of 23,875 would have
  dropped them at stage 1, we need to know that before trusting what it keeps.

### 3.4 Stage 2: the author-labelled model (Amendment 57), with four changes

The design in `docs/part2-library-search-plan.md` is sound and is the right middle stage: it is
the only way to apply a language-model signal to thousands of authors, and, unlike per-author
models, it can be checked on all the known books for a few dollars. Changes I would make:

1. **Widen the shortlist.** Amendment 57 scores each book against the 500 closest by each of
   two sweeps. On the saved ranks, that shortlist contains the true author 83% of the time.
   [repo] To pass the registered 70% bar the model would then need to be right on 84% of the
   books that reach it. Scoring is forward passes only: the plan's own figure is about an hour
   (about $4) for 1,000 authors per book, so a shortlist of about 4,000 (90% coverage) is about
   4 hours, about $12-15. The model's score for an author does not depend on the rest of the
   list, so a wider list cannot hurt the ones already in it.
2. **Take the shortlist from the fused, cleaned stage 1**, not from two raw sweeps.
3. **Report it as a ranker with coverage, as well as against the 70% bar.** The quantity the
   cascade needs is: among books whose author reached stage 2, how often is the author in the
   model's top 20, 50, 100? That sets K2.
4. **Two training seeds** (about $5 more), for the stability figure in section 2.

Optional, same training run [judgment, lower priority]: a second output on the same model that
directly guesses the author among all labels in one pass (a standard classifier head). It would
rank the whole library with no shortlist for almost no extra cost, and would be a second,
differently built language-model signal. It adds code and a new thing to explain, so only if
the owner wants it.

**Risk to name in the amendment:** authors with few blocks get weak labels. The named
candidates, if added as labels for the positive control, are the thinnest of all.

### 3.5 Stage 3: one model per author on the final list

**Cost.** Amendment 56 trained and scored 32 authors at three sizes for $6.83 [repo:
`docs/next.md`], about $0.20 an author; the library plan's own figure works out to about $0.40.
So 50 authors is about $10-20 at all three sizes, 200 authors about $40-80. The final list does
not have to be tiny.

**What we do not know: how good per-author models are at lists of 50.** "Best at picking the
author" was shown on lists of 3-4. Accuracy normally falls as lists grow (Luyckx and Daelemans
2011). And this list is the hardest possible one: its members were *selected* for resembling
the query. BOM-90 as proposed (lists of 8 and 12, drawn at random) would overstate stage 3's
accuracy. **Change to BOM-90:** score every benchmark test against all 55 benchmark authors'
models (list of 55), and also against each author's nearest neighbours by the sweep, so there
is a figure for hard lists. About $10-15, as the issue estimates, because the models must be
retrained either way.

### 3.6 How the errors compound, and the expected hit rate

A miss at any stage is final: an author dropped at stage 1 cannot be recovered. So the chance
of the true author finishing first is

> (reaches stage 2) × (kept by stage 2, given it got there) × (first at stage 3, given it got there).

**Do not multiply the headline rates of each stage.** The books that are hard for one method are
hard for the others (section 1.2). Each "given it got there" rate must be measured on the books
that actually survived the stage before, on the held-out half. Fisch et al. (2021) give the
formal version: assign each stage an allowed miss rate, and the total miss rate is at most
their sum.

**What we can say today.** Only the first factor is measured. The other two are assumptions,
shown as three scenarios so the arithmetic is visible. [first row repo; the rest judgment]

| | Cautious | Middle | Hopeful |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Reaches stage 2 (measured: 83% at 500 each, 88% at 1,000, 90% at 2,000) | 0.83 | 0.88 | 0.90 |
| Kept in stage 2's top 50, given it got there (not yet measured) | 0.70 | 0.85 | 0.95 |
| **True author is in the final list of 50** | **0.58** | **0.75** | **0.86** |
| First at stage 3, given it is on the list (91-94% at lists of 4; unknown at 50) | 0.60 | 0.75 | 0.90 |
| **True author finishes first** | **0.35** | **0.56** | **0.77** |

These are for an ordinary library book whose author is in the library. For the Book of Mormon,
multiply again by two things nobody can put a number on from inside the library: how much the
scriptural register costs (stage 1's cross-genre figure gives a partial reading), and the chance
that the author is in the library at all.

### 3.7 Was the author alive in 1828-29? A tag, not a filter

**The numbers** [repo; measured by the coordinating session today from `data/catalog/catalog.parquet`,
not re-run by me]: of 23,875 swept authors, 11,155 (47%) have life dates in the catalog's author
field. 5,299 (22% of all) died before 1828; 5,833 are known to have been alive; 12,720 (53%)
have no dates.

**Why it must not be a hard cut.** [judgment]

- Solomon Spalding died in 1816. The theory about him is that a dead man's manuscript was used.
  A cut on "alive" would remove a named candidate by rule.
- The registered plausibility gate already says the author "need not be alive": what matters
  there is that the text existed in print before mid-1829 (`docs/plausibility-gate.md`).
- The library is full of reprints. Bunyan (died 1688) and Swedenborg (died 1772) are in it.
  [repo] A dead author near the top is not an error; it is a different kind of finding.

**What a dead author near the top means.** Phase 1 already uses exactly these people: the
register placebo group is "died before 1 September 1827" (Amendment 49), writers who could not
have written the book. So a dead author who ranks high is evidence about **register or
influence**, to be read like the placebo writers, not a candidate for having written it. The
alive tag sorts the shortlist into two kinds of result instead of throwing one away.

**How to use it.**

- Tag every author: died before the cut-off; alive; born after; unknown. Report two lists side
  by side at every stage: **all authors**, and **not known to have died before the cut-off**
  (alive plus unknown). A third, **known alive**, is shown but flagged as covering under a
  quarter of the library.
- Apply the tag **at reporting, not at stage 1.** One run then serves every list, and an
  author's rank within a group is just their rank among that group's members.
- **One date, already registered.** The placebo rule uses 1 September 1827; the gate uses
  1 July 1829; the count above uses 1828. Pick one in the amendment. I suggest reusing the
  placebo date, since "could not have written it" is the same idea, and saying why.

**What it does to the numbers.**

- **Rivals:** 23,875 falls to about 18,580 not known dead (22% fewer), or to 5,833 known alive
  (76% fewer).
- **Accuracy:** removing rivals can only keep or improve the true author's rank. If the dead are
  spread evenly through the ranking, a true author at rank 10 moves to about rank 8, and top-1
  improves only on books where every author ahead is dead. So expect a small gain in the main
  list. The known-alive list would look much better (ranks divided by about four), but it is
  not a fair figure: it silently drops every true author who has no dates. [judgment]
- **Measuring it on the known books:** for each test book, drop rivals known to have died
  before that book's own first publication year, and recompute recall at K. Report it beside
  the unfiltered figure. Caveat to state: the true author nearly always passes this filter by
  construction (posthumous books aside), so the gain is real only to the extent that dead
  rivals were ahead.
- **List sizes** are recomputed per group, on the choosing books.

**The missing 53%.** Missing is not random: dates come with library authority headings, which
better-known authors have. Options:

1. **Leave "unknown" as its own group** (free; recommended now).
2. **Look up dates only for authors who reach the final lists** (a few hundred at most), by the
   same documented research the plausibility gate already does. Small, and it is where the tag
   matters.
3. **Bulk lookup of all 12,720** against the Library of Congress Name Authority File, VIAF or
   Wikidata. This is an outside fetch and needs the owner's approval first. It sends only names
   of long-dead published authors, nothing private. Name matching will be wrong for common
   names, which are exactly the split-label cases, so do it after stage 0 if at all. I would
   not do it before seeing whether option 2 is enough.

## 4. The handoff to Phase 1

### 4.1 Keep the registered wall between finding and testing

The pre-registration (section 3) already says how a sweep lead becomes a candidate: it must beat
the lead threshold, pass the plausibility gate, be added by a dated amendment, and then be
tested on the **confirmation half** of the Book of Mormon only, "which the sweep never sees".
[repo] Two consequences:

- **The search uses the discovery half only**, as the existing sweep did. Amendment 57's text
  does not say which half the labelled model scores; the next amendment should.
- **The rerun is judged on the confirmation half.** Authors picked because they resemble a text
  will resemble that same text again. Scoring them on passages the search never saw is the
  standard protection against that.

### 4.2 What the shortlist is called

The existing sweep found 0 leads by the registered threshold. A cascade's top 50 is therefore
not "50 leads". The amendment should give the list its own name and status: **exploratory
comparison authors**, chosen by rank, not by passing a threshold. A member becomes a
confirmatory candidate only by the registered rule. Otherwise the list quietly lowers a bar set
in advance.

### 4.3 The rerun, step by step

For each author on the final list (and for the eight named candidates, in the same run):

1. **Sample:** the author's sweep profile blocks (up to 20,000 words), King James masked, any
   Book of Mormon quotation removed (Amendment 43's rule; it will rarely apply before 1830).
2. **Per-author models at all three sizes**, scored on the confirmation half, as Phase 1.
3. **Equal dose** (Amendment 45): every author cut to slices of exactly the revelation sample's
   3,039 tokens, up to 12 slices (Amendment 51). This is the fair comparison. A library author
   with 20,000 words against a 3,039-token sample will otherwise win on size alone.
4. **Placebo and register check** (Amendments 49, 51): the same writers who could not have
   written it, in the same pool. Dead authors on the shortlist join this group by the tag in
   section 3.7, reported separately from the registered placebo list.
5. **A register reference among the shortlist itself.** Mark which shortlisted authors write in
   scriptural or prophetic style (the BOM-54 curation and genre labels exist). Report each
   author's standing among authors of the same style, as the earlier report's reference-group
   idea proposed.
6. **Cross-work recognition** (Amendment 49's decoys), where the author has two or more works.
   Two thirds of the library's authors have one work [repo], so for many this will read "not
   testable", and Phase 1's caution applies: the model has learned a book, not a person.
7. **Plausibility gate** (Amendment 40), blind to the scores, for every author on the list, not
   only the top ones, so that the gate cannot be bent toward a favourite.
8. **Seeds:** as Phase 1.

### 4.4 What "better candidates" would and would not mean

**Would mean:** the library holds writers whose text the tools place as close to the Book of
Mormon as the eight named candidates' text, or closer, under equal dose, at more than one model
size, on passages the search never saw; and some of them pass the gate. That would show the
eight-person list was not the best comparison set, and it would give the closest sample (the
revelations) a far larger field to be measured against, which makes that result either stronger
or weaker in a way eight candidates cannot.

**Would not mean:**

- **That any of them wrote it.** Closeness among those tested is all these tools measure.
- **That the author is on the list.** "None of these" remains impossible for every method here
  (outline 5.4). A longer list makes this more important, not less: with thousands of authors,
  someone is always closest.
- **That style has been separated from register.** Writers who imitate scripture will rank high.
  Expect the shortlist to be dominated by sermons, prophecy, and pseudo-biblical works, and
  expect dead authors among them. The placebo result at 590M (Ilive and Dowling reach the
  revelations) is the warning already in hand.

**Equally informative outcome:** nobody in 23,875 comes near the revelations under equal dose.
That would not show who wrote the book either. It would show that the Phase 1 result is not an
accident of a short list.

**An awkward fact to state up front.** The person whose text Phase 1 found closest is not in the
library, and nor are most of the named candidates; their samples are far smaller than any
library author's. A library search can only find people who published 10,000 words in
1780-1830 that survive in these collections.

Step 9 needs about as much again.

**Why step 2 and 3 cannot wait:** the labels and the held-out books are part of the labelled
model's training set. BOM-89 step 1 (building that set) is next in the queue. If it is built on
today's labels and today's 200 books, fixing either later costs a retraining run.

### Not worth doing

- **Running the cheap sweeps repeatedly.** Same answer every time.
- **A model for each of 23,875 authors** (about $10,000), or the 590M version of stage 2 before
  the 297M result is in.
- **Learned fusion weights, or any combiner trained on the 200 books.** Too few books; see
  Amendment 55.
- **A tournament over sub-groups.** Same winner as a full ranking for our scores; worse for the
  word-count methods.
- **Chasing 95% coverage at stage 1.** It takes about 6,800 authors per sweep, which is no
  longer a shortlist. Fix the labels and find out what the hard books are instead.
- **A hard "alive in 1828" cut,** or a bulk outside lookup of 12,720 life dates before knowing
  whether dates for the final list are enough.
- **Modern rerankers or style vectors.** They have read the book.
- **Using General Imposters as a library-wide signal.** It reorders a top-100 only.

## 6. What I would change in the current plan

1. **Amendment 57's shortlist (500 by each sweep) caps the model at 83%** on the saved ranks.
   Widen it to about 90% coverage and take it from the fused list.
2. **Judge the search as a list-maker, not only a name-giver.** Keep the 70% top-1 bar and
   report it; add coverage at K, which is what a cascade needs.
3. **Clean labels and fix the test books before the training set is built.**
4. **Hold out books for every choice.** Nothing chosen on the 200 should be scored on the 200.
5. **Say which half of the Book of Mormon the search sees** (the discovery half), and judge the
   rerun on the other.
6. **Name the shortlist "exploratory comparison authors"** so the registered lead threshold is
   not lowered by the back door.
7. **Make BOM-90 bigger and harder**; the cascade's last stage depends on it.
8. **Put the named candidates into the gallery as a positive control.**
9. **Use one cut-off date for "could not have written it"** across the placebo rule and the
   alive tag.

## Appendix: how the [repo] figures were computed

All from `data/sweep/calibration.json` `records` (per book: `mfw_rank`, `style_rank`) unless
noted. No corpus text was read; names and counts only.

- **Recall at K, "both sweeps, lists joined":** share of the 200 books with
  `min(mfw_rank, style_rank) <= K`. The joined list can hold up to 2K authors.
- **Conformal list size at coverage c:** the `ceil(201 × c)`-th smallest of that minimum rank.
- **Shared misses:** books with `mfw_rank > 1000` (44), `style_rank > 1000` (28), both (24);
  expected if unrelated, 44 × 28 / 200 = 6.2. Correlation of the two ranks across books
  (Spearman): 0.85.
- **Split-half:** 2,000 random 100/100 splits (seed 0); K is the target quantile of the minimum
  rank on one half, recall measured on the other; ranges are the 5th to 95th percentile.
- **Split labels:** cataloguing suffixes and authority numbers stripped from
  `authors.parquet` `author`; labels sharing the remainder counted as one group. Crude on
  purpose; a floor, with some false joins.
- **Worst books:** minimum rank above 1,000, cross-tabulated with `chunks` from the records and
  the count of `calib_profile` blocks and distinct works per author in `blocks.parquet`.
- **Interim older methods:** ranks parsed from the 50 per-book lines in `logs/a57_older.log` at
  the time of writing. Not final and not used for any figure above except the remark that the
  three word-count methods miss the same books (9 of 50 missed at 1,000 by all three; 11 by
  Cosine Delta alone).
- **Per-author model cost:** $6.83 for Amendment 56's 32 authors at three sizes
  (`docs/next.md`); about $10,000 for 23,875 authors (`docs/part2-library-search-plan.md`).
- **Life dates:** supplied by the coordinating session (catalog `author` field joined on
  `author_norm`); not recomputed here.
